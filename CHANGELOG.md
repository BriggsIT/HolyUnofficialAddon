## 0.32.0: third and fourth topics, new languages
Two new research items give your games more topics, eleven new languages open over the years, and the game design
screen grows a row to fit them.
- **Layered Settings** (Other research, from 1988, after the game's Sub topic research) gives a third topic;
  **Rich Worlds** (from 1996) a fourth.
- **Third topic and Fourth topic** on the game design screen: a row under Main topic and Subtopic that works the same
  way. Locked, with the research to do as the tooltip, until Layered Settings (third) and Rich Worlds (fourth) are
  done. Each button's tooltip says whether the topic suits the genre and goes with the game's other topics, the review
  change, and the extra work.
- **The review:** a topic that suits the genre and goes with the others lifts it, one that doesn't lowers it (from
  about -6% to +5% altogether). The review says how the mix works. Topics are judged by their themes: Crime, City and
  Cars go together; Horror and Kittens don't.
- **The work:** each extra topic adds about 10% to the development cost and time.
- Sequels and spin-offs start with the original's extra topics; ports and remasters keep them. Contract games and
  studio games have two topics, as before. Landmarks count all four topics.
- **Eleven new languages:** Portuguese, Dutch, Greek, Hungarian, Czech, Turkish, Romanian, Ukrainian, Arabic, Hindi
  and Indonesian, each from the year its market grew (Dutch 1980 to Hindi and Indonesian 2005). A second row of flags
  under the game's eleven on the design screen. Each adds 1-2% to the game's sales while it's on the market (the
  mirror of what the game takes off for each of its own languages a game lacks) and costs the game's price for a
  translation (free for your studio's own). Add-ons, budget and GOTY editions and ports carry their main game's.
- **Translate the Game:** a new update in Updates & Addons brings a released game out in new languages it doesn't
  have yet.
- **The taller design screen:** the gameplay features list grows into the space, and the platform page shows the
  timed exclusive choice (which platform first, for how long, what the deal pays) with its own icon, instead of a
  pop-up.
- **Market Check** (design focus page): the trend and what's out of fashion, your genre's popularity, whether your
  genre and topics are on trend, and which studios are making something similar right now.
- **Project Overview** (financing page): the cost and work with the extra topics, roughly how many months at your
  recent pace, the topic mix's review change, the new languages, a landmark the game could become, and the timed
  exclusive.

## 0.31.0: landmark games
Historical games you can make first, before the studio that made them for real.
- **22 landmarks from 1976 to 1989**, from Breakout and Space Invaders to SimCity and Populous, on The Holy Expansion's
  real franchise list. Each has a genre and topics it needs (written for the addon: Holy's list files many games
  loosely), and a quality bar: its real review score less 10, at least 70%.
- **Claiming one:** when a new game of yours is finished and fits a landmark (its genre and topics, a good enough
  projected review, and the years), a pop-up comes up before you choose to release it or keep developing. Make it the
  landmark and it's renamed, shows the landmark's pictures, and its studio never makes it; a studio that was making it
  shelves its version. A landmark can be made from 3 years before its real year until its studio releases it (or 5
  years after its real year, if nobody does).
- **At release:** IP points, fans of its genre, hype, and its genre becomes the trend. Your second landmark in a genre
  that isn't your special genre offers to make it your special genre.
- **Studios make them for real:** each landmark's studio makes it on time, in the right genre, and is known for that
  genre from then on.
- **Later entries belong to their series:** The Holy Expansion's list has later games of a series as series of their
  own (Mega Man X, Metal Gear Solid, Ocarina of Time), so their studio started each as a new series, even when someone
  else owned the original, or you did. Now they're made as sequels (or spin-offs, in another genre) by whoever owns
  the series, around their real year. Your series: no studio makes them. A series whose owner has closed is taken
  over by the studio of its next entry. Sequels after one continue its name ("Metal Gear Solid 2"). Series already
  started in a save stay as they are.
- **Landmarks tab** in the Industry window: the landmarks made so far, who made them and when. The rest are for you
  to discover; "Show upcoming landmarks" in Game settings lists them all, with what it takes to make each.
- **The pop-ups** show the landmark's screenshot across the top, what it takes (or what you got) as a row of facts,
  and blur and dim the screen behind them. Yours coming out gets a fanfare.
- **Pictures:** a folder per landmark (PNG or JPG, any size; cropped to 4:3). Landmarks without pictures show the
  game's usual screenshot.

## 0.30.0: every event on real systems
The last events that only paid money or nudged sales by a flat percentage now change the game's real systems, like
the rest. Two of the game's own mechanics do most of the work:
- **Saturation:** the game tracks how close each boxed game is to having sold to everyone who wants it, and its weekly
  sales shrink as it gets there. Bringing lapsed buyers back lowers it; renting, typing in or second-hand copies raise
  it, so the game runs out of buyers sooner.
- **Real copies:** a rental chain's or a card maker's order is counted as sales of the game (its totals, revenue and
  charts), from your warehouse when you publish it boxed.

**Decisions**
- **A Copy of Your Game:** win the lawsuit and the court takes their copy off the shelves (as well as damages). Outsell
  It gives hype. License It makes theirs the official version: a fee, and 15% of what it earns for a year. Ignore It
  leaves both in the shops, crowding the same genre and topic as any two games would.
- **A Harsh Review:** writing to the editor can get the game re-reviewed (its real review score goes up 2-3 points, and
  every magazine's review follows), or readers side with the magazine and its user score drops. The advert gives hype.
- **Shelf Space:** outbidding them buys a display (hype). Losing the shelf shortens the game's shelf life (saturation).
- **A High-Score Contest:** fans, IP points and lapsed players coming back; the national final adds hype.
- **Rent It Out?:** the chain buys copies at a rental premium. Renters become fans, but they won't buy it.
- **Stuck on Your Game:** parents complaining about the hint line cost user score; free tips raise it and keep players
  playing.
- **A Cartridge Shortage:** your production room is affected. Pay the premium (copies cost 50% more to make for 8
  weeks), ship what you can (no new copies for 8 weeks: the warehouse runs down and orders wait, as the game handles
  any shortage), or share your chips (no copies for 12 weeks, and the other studio launches sooner).
- **A Distributor in Trouble:** taking your stock back puts the copies back in your warehouse.
- **A Pocket Version:** royalties follow how well known your series is (the game's IP fame), not a coin toss.
- **A Cartoon:** an animated series of your series, premiering about a year later and judged on the series' fame and
  its games' reviews. A hit brings players back to every game of the series, plus IP points and fans; a flop costs IP
  points. With The Holy Expansion's Movie & TV deals on, it is Holy's own deal (so the two never double up).
  Co-producing also brings the merchandise: your fan shop's demand for the series starts fresh at the premiere.
- **A Graphics Card Bundle:** the card maker buys a copy for every card, at a bundle price. New players become fans and
  judge the graphics in the user score.
- **A Cassette in the Post:** the game they send is a small studio's real game in development. Publish It puts it in
  your publishing offers, the game's own, to negotiate and publish like any other (within your studio level's limit).
- **Concerned Parents:** defending it on TV gives hype and fans, but the row may get it re-rated a step higher.
- **Hidden Content:** recalling it scraps your warehouse stock of it, and players respect you for owning up.
- **CD Burners:** taking the losses means your copy protection stays weaker: that is the loss.
- **Print Your Code:** readers who type it in won't buy it (saturation).
- **The Mod Team** without Holy's Communities: supporting the modders brings lapsed players back.
- **Competitors:** out-marketing is hype; the better-reviewed of two launches gets the attention (hype, fans); in a
  price war, holding your price loses buyers to their cheaper game for good; making your game stand out adds polish
  (gameplay points for its review) and hype; and a rushed game that beats theirs out gets the launch buzz (hype).

**Headlines**
- **Budget re-releases:** bargain hunters bring buyers back to your games over a year old.
- **A new chain of shops:** an opening order for your boxed games, filled from your warehouse.
- **The dot-com boom and bust:** interest in MMOs and free-to-play games rises, then falls; with The Holy Expansion's
  stock market and your company public, your share price follows. In the bust, one or two of the weakest young
  studios close for real (their series can come up at the Closure Auction).
- **Internet cafés:** interest in online games grows, and your esports leagues (Holy) draw bigger crowds.
- **Used games:** second-hand copies take the buyers of boxed games over six months old (saturation).
- **The handheld revival:** other makers' handhelds sell far more machines (their installed base grows 15%).

**A Console Maker Quits** is now rare, a pop-up, and only happens to a maker that's really struggling. In 0.29.0
  it was a headline that was all but certain between 2002 and 2005, and it always took out the newest console maker:
  Microsoft, whose first Xbox starts with the smallest market share. (The game's data already retires Sega on
  schedule: the Dreamcast is its last console.)
  - Rolled once a year from 2000 to 2009 at 4%, and only if a maker's newest console has been out for 3 years or
    more and its consoles' combined market share is under 10% and the lowest, with at least two other makers left.
  - A pop-up with choices:
    - **Hire Their Engineers:** two strong Technicians (hardware and engineering) join the job market.
    - **Port Your Games:** when you have a game on its consoles and a dev kit for another maker's console, that maker
      pays half of porting it there, if you start the port within a year (Updates & Addons).
    - **Carry On.**
  - Its own studio goes third-party: its games come out on every console from then on.
- Saves hit by 0.29.0's version get their consoles back: the consoles it cancelled get their launch dates back
  from the game's data. One whose date has passed is announced at once and comes out the next month (the game only
  launches a console in the month of its date).

## 0.29.0: events for the 3D and online years
A new pack of industry events for 1995-2009. Like 0.28.0's, every one changes the game's real systems: engines,
IPs, studios, platforms, copy protection, age ratings and Holy's modding communities, not just money and sales.

- **Engine Request** (from 1993): a friendly studio wants one of your engines that you don't sell.
  - **License It to Them:** they pay a fee now and build their next game on your engine. The fee and share come
    from the game's own engine market: more than the dearest engine on sale, for a deal only they get. The game's
    own engine statement pays your share of that game's profit when it leaves the shops.
  - **Put It on Sale:** the engine goes on sale to every studio at the going rate, a price and share studios will
    actually pay (the game has them turn down engines dearer than the market's).
  - **Keep It In-House.**
- **The Mod Team** (from 1995): fans made a hit mod of your game. With The Holy Expansion's Communities, this is
  Holy's "Mod of the month" for one of your games; without them, one of your well-loved games now and then.
  - **Hire Them:** its two leads join the job market as a strong designer and programmer.
  - **Support Them:** IP points, and Holy's official modding tools for the game (more modders if it has them), at
    what Holy charges for its modding tools.
  - **Make It Official:** buy the mod and release it as a real paid add-on, out now; the modders get 20% for a year.
    It's priced and rated like the game's own add-ons: the mod counts as two kinds of add-on content, so it costs
    what the game's add-on screen would charge to make that (a share of the main game's development cost), and sells
    with that add-on quality. Like any add-on, it lifts the main game's sales too.
  - **Leave Them to It.**
- **Community Work** for Support rooms (with Holy's Communities on): a toggle in the room's menu. On, the room starts
  Holy's modding tools and tournaments for your games by itself when they're ready, pausing calls while it works and
  going back to the phones when it's done.
- **A Star Leaves** (from 1990): one of your best people (a skill of 70+) leaves to found a studio. It's a real new
  studio that saves, makes games and shows in the Industry window, with one of the addon's company logos that nobody
  uses and the name that goes with it.
  - **Wish Them Well:** it starts friendly.
  - **Back Them:** costs money, which goes into the studio's company value; it starts stronger and as an ally.
  - **Make Them Stay:** a 40% raise. About half stay; the rest leave bitter, and the new studio starts against you.
- **Closure Auction** (from 1985): a closed studio's best series is auctioned. It follows the closure rather than
  coming up at random: as soon as the series is free to change hands under the game's own IP-market rule (none of
  its games still in the shops), and nothing else is waiting. Closures from the last two years count, each studio is
  auctioned once, and there's at most one auction every three months. Win it and the series is yours, as if bought
  on the IP market: sequels, remasters and spin-offs.
  - **Bid the Asking Price:** about a 55% chance.
  - **Bid High:** 40% more, almost certain.
  - **Pass:** the other bidder, a real studio, gets the series.
- **Cover Disc** (1994-2008): a magazine wants a demo of your game in development. A demo gives a big hype boost,
  but about a third of the time reviewers remember it and its review score drops 3-6 points at release. A trailer
  costs money and gives half the hype.
- **Graphics Card Bundle** (1996-2005): a 3D-card maker wants to bundle your new PC game. They pay a fee and PC sales
  rise 12% for 12 weeks, unless its graphics review is weak. Weak graphics get shown up: only 6%, and players
  grumble. Ask for more and they may bundle another studio's game instead.
- **Fund a Port** (from 1995): the maker of a platform you have a dev kit for offers to pay half of porting your game
  to it (three quarters if you push your luck). Start the port in Updates & Addons within a year, and the discount
  is there in the port's price.
- **CD Burners** (1998-2005): every copy protection on the market loses 15 points of strength.
  - **Update Your Copy Protection:** starts your own copy protection's update in a free room.
  - **Patch Your Games:** with Holy, a quarter of your PC games' pirated copies stop working.
  - **Take the Losses:** your PC games sell 8% less for 12 weeks.
- **Hidden Content** (from 1998): fans find a cut scene stronger than your game's age rating allows.
  - **Accept a Higher Rating:** the rating really goes up.
  - **Recall and Patch:** a short sales dip.
  - **Deny It:** a gamble; you may be re-rated anyway, and players feel lied to.
- **New headlines:** sales effects on the real games out at the time.
  - The dot-com boom (1999-2000) and bust (2001-2002) for online games, and internet cafés.
  - The used-games market (old boxed games sell less) and the handheld revival.
  - **A console maker quits hardware** (2000-2009, once): the weakest maker with a machine still to come stops making
    consoles. Its consoles are discontinued, and its next one never comes out.
- **Fix:** the "sells out at launch" headline no longer praises a console that The Holy Expansion gave a weak launch.
- **Fix:** a studio that closes no longer leaves its unfinished game "in development" forever. The game stops a
  closed studio's development, so that game could never come out, and it kept the studio's series off the IP market
  for good. It's now cancelled, the way the game drops an NPC game it can't make (announced ones get a news line).
  This also clears games stuck this way in existing saves.
- **Fix:** genre names ending in "Game" read as plurals where they stand for the genre's games: a studio "loves
  Role-Playing Games", "Best sellers in Sports Games" in the charts, and "Role-Playing MMOs" (not "Role-Playing
  Game MMOs") in MMO anti-bot systems.

## 0.28.0: events that follow through
Events now change the game's real systems, not just money and sales numbers.

- **Publishers Bid is binding**, like the game's own exclusive publishing contract: when the game is done, its
  screen offers "Publish with <them>", which publishes it with them straight away (whatever its review score), and
  self-publishing is greyed out with the reason. The event only bids on games a publisher can take (not handheld,
  arcade, free-to-play or add-ons). Signing an exclusive publishing contract with someone else ends the deal: the
  advance goes back, with no hard feelings.
- **The arch-rival's Release Clash is real:** their actual next game is moved onto your release week. Delay yours
  (theirs gets the week), hold the date, or out-market them (an 8-point edge). Once both are out, the reviews decide.
- **The arch-rival's Copycat is real:** their next game becomes a copy of your hit (same genre and topic) and comes
  out within a couple of months. Win the lawsuit and they have to rework it and come out months late; otherwise
  the reviews decide when it lands. If the copy reviews better, it takes 15% of your sales for 12 weeks.
- **A results pop-up** for every release clash and copycat, rivals' and competitors': both games side by side with
  their studios, review scores and verdicts, a press quote from one of that year's outlets saying which is better,
  and what it does to sales.
- **The Game of the Film uses the game's own film licences:** a film studio offers one of the game's film licences
  that's out by now and suits your game's genre (or at least doesn't clash with it), at the licence market's price
  for one game. Your game carries the licence, and the game applies it as it does any licence: better reviews and
  more sales for as long as it sells (most when the film suits the genre), shown on the game. A weak film helps
  little. Sharing the revenue instead costs a share tied to the film's quality (5% plus 1% for every 5 quality
  points), for a year after release. The event no longer adds a sales boost or hype of its own.
- **Cabinet Deal** makes the game's real arcade version: the home game gets the game's own arcade-version bonus, and
  you can't port it to arcade yourself.
- **Same Idea, Rush It** really finishes sooner: it cuts the last gameplay features still to be made (about a fifth
  of the remaining work), and the game ships without their quality. Out before the competitor's game, it gets
  +10% sales for 8 weeks.
- **Share a Stand?** is tied to July's trade fair: book your stand as usual, and the other studio pays half and
  shows its game next to yours. Skip the fair and the deal lapses (they're not pleased).
- **Concerned Parents, milder version:** really lowers the age rating, up to two steps and never below what the
  topic allows (an 18+ game can become 12+, not 6+). The game's own rating sales effect does the rest.
- **A Cartridge Shortage** and **A Distributor in Trouble** only come up for boxed games you publish yourself.
- **Who declares you its rival is no longer always the same studio.** "A studio your size" measured your size by your
  studio's star rating, which the game leaves at 1 star forever, so the weakest developer always won (Midway, in
  save after save). Your size now comes from your studio level, and the pick is a draw among every developer that
  isn't an ally: likelier the closer it is to your size, three times as likely if it's one of your competitors, and
  likelier the worse you get on. Competitors' "your size / bigger / smaller" uses the same measure.

## 0.27.0: balance patch
A second balance review, covering everything added since 0.18 (sales events, the convention, industry events,
rivals and competitors), checked against real saves from 1976 to 2008.

- **Launches start when the game ships, not when you pick a release date.** The game counts a game with a release
  date as out from the day you pick the date, but sells nothing until the date. Holy's launch boost (up to +120%
  over 4 weeks), your store's launch boost and every "after release" event effect used to run out during
  pre-orders. They now wait for the release date.
- **Caps:**
  - Industry events add at most +-40% to one game's sales, so they can't fill Holy's whole cap on their own.
  - Without The Holy Expansion, your games' sales boosts now stay within Holy's range (-50%..+250%); before,
    late-game boosts could multiply sales about 4.5x, and penalties could stop a game selling at all.
  - Other studios' games: the addon's effects on them (front-page spots, sales, release clashes, price wars) now
    apply with Holy installed too (Holy only boosts your games), within -40%..+100%.
  - Your store adds at most 50,000 wishlists to Holy's Launch Planner per game.
- **Event money grows with your studio.** Holy's price curve rises about 5x from 1980 to 2008; a studio's income
  can rise 2,000x. Event amounts about one of your games are now priced from what your games of that size earn
  (the old amounts stay as the minimum, so the early game is unchanged): the film licence, out-marketing and
  Outsell It, adverts, guide and cabinet fees, the clone lawsuit, the Concerned Parents choices. Caps tied to a
  game's weekly sales (shelf space, the cartridge premium, the distributor, rentals) grow with last year's income.
- **Publishers Bid:** the advance follows the game's own publisher guarantee (review x size x difficulty), up to a
  tenth of what your games of that size earn: about $0.5-13M late game instead of $0.2M.
- **Drive Them Out** costs 1-1.25x the studio's company value, instead of a fixed $20-150M in every year.
- **A Studio Needs Help:** funding costs a fifth of what their games earn, and there's a 25% chance they still
  can't finish (it's shelved and the money is gone).
- **The Game of the Film:** sharing the revenue now takes 10% for 26 weeks, with the sales boost lasting the same
  26 weeks (it was 15% for a boost of only 8).
- **Price War:** Match It is a real price cut on your game for 8 weeks (sales up by half the cut), and their price
  really drops too. Around a 20% cut, matching and holding come out about even.
- **Convention:** tickets, merch and booths pay about what a sold-out event costs. The hype, fans and studio points
  are the reason to hold one, not the money (it used to make 1.6-2.4x its cost).
- **No more one-sided choices:** royalties (pocket version, cabinets, hint line) pay about 1.3-1.4x the fee if they
  catch on and much less if not; paid lessons train up to 5 people; licensing a copy of your game lets its official
  version take some of your sales; renting a hit out pays about what it costs in sales.
- **Smaller:**
  - Genre crazes and fatigue come about every 40 weeks (twice as often as the game's own trend before).
  - A Copy of Your Game and the arch-rival's Copycat keep 12 weeks apart.
  - Console storefronts have running costs (15% of their commission).
  - The development-cost cap behind updates grows with the years from 2008.
  - Finances: refunds reduce the cost they came from instead of counting as Misc income; exclusive deals count
    as Licenses; paid lessons as training.
  - The convention's showcase says "up to +X%": it fades over its 4 weeks.
  - With Sales Events off, your store's sale no longer adds a second boost while Holy's Seasonal Sale runs.
- **Bottom bar fix:** on some PCs the addon's buttons (and the arrow) were missing. They were added a moment before
  the game laid out its own button row, kept the wrong anchor, and ended up just below the screen. They now always
  line up with the game's buttons, whenever the row is ready.
- **Event fixes:**
  - A Publishers Bid answered after the game came out pays nothing (it used to pay and leave a deal that never
    settled).
  - Closing or ignoring a pop-up never takes a choice you can't afford; the arch-rival's Release Clash, left
    alone, holds your date instead of delaying your game.
  - Raises from events no longer compound with the sandbox salary setting.
  - Applicants from events have the profession, skills and perks they're meant to.
  - Events no longer pick a studio's publishing offer or contract work as "their next game".
  - A New Machine Wants Games: if you bought the kit while the offer waited, there's no promise to keep.
  - The Game of the Film only picks games without a licence.

## 0.26.0: competitors
- **Competitors** (the rival merge, step 2): about four studios that meet you most in the market right now, picked
  again every year and after each of your releases. They make games in your genres, are about your size, and have
  something out or coming. Competing isn't hating: an ally can be a competitor.
  - **Release Clash:** a competitor's game in your genre lands with yours. With a release date set (pre-orders),
    their game really is moved onto your week, and once both are out the better-reviewed one sells 10% more for
    6 weeks and the other 10% less. Delay yours 3 weeks, hold the date, or out-market it (an 8-point edge).
    Released straight away, it's their game out the same fortnight: out-market it, or let the reviews decide.
  - **Talent Race:** a competitor offers one of your good people (not your very best: that's the arch-rival's
    move) a job. Match it with a 15% raise, or let them go.
  - **Price War:** a competitor cuts the price of the game fighting yours. Match it (sales hold, less per copy for
    8 weeks) or hold your price (sales -12% for 6 weeks). Only for games you publish yourself.
  - **Same Idea:** a competitor announces a game in the same genre (sometimes the same topic) as yours in
    development. Rush it (crunch: staff motivation down, first to the shops), make it stand out (marketing), or
    ignore it.
  - At most one competitor fight every 8 weeks. Fights that go badly cost standing, so a competitor you keep
    clashing with can turn into a rival, and in time your arch-rival.
  - The arch-rival's Release Clash and a competitor's never land in the same weeks.
  - Shelf Space, A Copy of Your Game and the hit headlines pick a competitor first when one fits.
  - **Industry window:** a Competitors stat card (hover it for who and why), a "competitor" tag and a Competitors
    filter in the studio list, and a line on their page saying why they compete with you.
